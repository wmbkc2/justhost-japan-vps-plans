## What a Tokyo location buys you, and what it doesn't

Japan sits at the end of the shortest trans-Pacific routes, and Tokyo is the best-connected node in Northeast Asia. Published figures from a competitor's Japan page (dated May 2025, so treat them as indicative rather than current market truth) put Seoul under 30 ms, Shanghai under 50 ms, Hong Kong under 55 ms, Singapore under 75 ms and the US West Coast at 100–120 ms. If your users are in Japan, Korea or northern China, a Tokyo box removes an ocean from the round trip.

There's a second reason people buy Japanese IPs that has nothing to do with latency. Google.co.jp factors server location into Japanese-language ranking, some Japanese payment gateways and CDN origin rules only accept domestic IPs, and hosting data on a Japan-resident node simplifies data-handling obligations under Japan's APPI. For a shop selling into Japan, that's often the deciding factor.

Who actually benefits: sites and stores aimed at Japanese customers, Japan-only services that geo-restrict by IP, game servers for players in Japan, Korea and Taiwan, dev and test environments that need to sit near Tokyo, and proxies or VPN endpoints for a Japan-facing audience. Who doesn't: anyone whose users are all in Europe. A Frankfurt or Helsinki box will beat Tokyo on latency for that audience every time, and no amount of Tokyo peering changes the distance.

## Six things to check before you pay

**1. Whether the IP is actually seen as Japanese.** A third-party test of the Tokyo TYO2 node in April 2026 reported IPv4 geolocating to the UAE in its checks and IPv6 to the US, even though the server sits in Tokyo. Geolocation databases disagree with each other constantly, and no host can guarantee a specific verdict. If a service whitelists Japanese IPs, deploy first, test the exact lookup that service uses, then decide. Reviews of this provider report IP changes being free from the control panel, which makes a bad IP an annoyance rather than a dealbreaker.

**2. What "unmetered" means.** It's a port cap, not infinite bandwidth: 300 Mbit fair-share on the two small plans, 500 Mbit on Vega, 750 Mbit above that. The page translates those into roughly 93 TB, 155 TB and 232 TB a month. "Fair-share" means you share that port, so sustained saturation at peak hours is not guaranteed to hold.

**3. IPv6.** The constructor offers IPv4 + IPv6 and IPv6-only configurations, so both stacks are available. Given the geolocation results above, verify the v6 prefix behaves the way your application expects before you build around it.

**4. Windows.** The plans and templates I could verify are Linux-oriented. Nothing on the pricing page confirms a Windows licence option, so if you need RDP, ask support before paying.

**5. Refund terms.** Neither the pricing page nor the homepage FAQ states a money-back window. Confirm it in the checkout terms or with support, and weight that heavily if you're considering a 12-month payment on a plan you haven't tested.

**6. Routing into mainland China.** The April 2026 Tokyo test found all three Chinese carriers on international routes, some traffic transiting the US, with degraded performance at peak and a recommendation to use transit. If China Telecom/CU/CMI optimisation is your reason for buying, this isn't the product for it.
